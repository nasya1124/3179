01

Where do Australia's forests grow?

Here, “forest” means land where trees over two metres shade
at least a fifth of the ground.

Tasmania is the most densely forested state.

This map shades each state by how much of its land is covered by native forest, so darker states are more heavily forested. Tasmania stands out, with native forest covering half of the state. The small Australian Capital Territory is even higher, at 55%.

The inland states are much lighter. Forest covers less than 10% of South Australia and Western Australia, where much of the land is too dry for forest to grow.

Shading shows forest as a share of land area, so large and small states can be compared fairly. One hectare is a square 100 metres on each side.

But Queensland holds the most forest.

When forest is measured by total area instead of share, the order changes. Queensland has 52 million hectares of native forest, which is 39% of all the native forest in Australia.

Tasmania, the most heavily forested state on the map, drops to second-last here. This shows that a small state can be densely forested but still hold only a small part of the country's forest.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 1.1a.i (2023); ABS ASGS Edition 4 boundaries (2026).

02

What are the forests made of?

Next, what is the forest made of, and why do
some trees grow in only one region?

Three in four hectares are eucalypt.

Eucalypts, also known as gum trees, make up 77% of Australia's native forest. That is about 101 million hectares, or three out of every four hectares of forest.

Acacia, which Australians call wattle, comes a distant second at 8%, followed by melaleuca, or paperbark, at 5%. Rainforest makes up less than 3% of the total.

Five smaller types are grouped as “Other types”. Hover that slice to see them.

Some trees live in just one corner.

While eucalypts grow across the country, some trees are only found in one region. This map shows where three well-known species have been recorded.

Mountain ash, the tallest flowering plant in the world, is found almost only in Victoria and Tasmania. Bunya pine is mostly found in south-east Queensland, and the boab grows in the Kimberley, in the far north-west.

Each circle counts herbarium specimens, which are plant samples collected and stored by scientists. Counts are grouped into grid squares about 100 km wide. They show where a species has been recorded, not how many trees there are. Click a species in the legend to show only its records.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 1.1a.i (2023); Atlas of Living Australia herbarium records.

03

How much forest is lost and regained?

Every year some forest is cleared, some grows back on
cleared land, and some is planted for timber.

Uncleared forest is rarely cleared now. Regrowth is cleared instead.

Clearing of previously uncleared forest has dropped by 96%, from 605,000 hectares in 1989–90 to just 22,000 hectares in 2020–21. This means forest that had not been cleared before is now rarely cleared.

Regrowth tells a different story. Regrowth is forest that has grown back on land that was cleared in the past, and much of it is cleared again. In 2020–21, re-clearing removed 155,000 hectares, about seven times more than first-time clearing.

The cream line shows the overall change each year. Forest area grew for only seven years in a row, from 2008–09 to 2014–15.

Click a legend entry to highlight one type.

A hardwood boom, then a decline.

Not all forest is native. Plantations are trees planted as a crop to produce timber. They are either hardwood, which is mostly eucalypt, or softwood, which is mostly pine.

Hardwood plantations grew tenfold during the 1990s and 2000s, reaching nearly 1 million hectares in 2008–09. Since then, they have shrunk by 31%. Softwood has stayed steady at about 1 million hectares throughout.

Drag across the lower strip to zoom. Click a legend entry or a band to highlight that type in both charts. Starts in 1973–74, when hardwood reporting became consistent.

Most states plant pine; Tasmania and the west plant gums.

Each year, about 55,000 hectares of plantation are planted or replanted after harvest. The type of tree planted depends a lot on the state.

In Queensland and New South Wales, almost all new planting is softwood. Tasmania, and Western Australia together with the Northern Territory, do the opposite. About three-quarters of their planting is hardwood.

Each bar shows shares, not size. Hover a bar for each state's hectares.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 1.1a.iv (2023), Figures 1.1a.iv-3 and 1.1a.iv-4, from Australia's National Inventory Report 2021; ABARES Indicator 2.1e, annual averages 2016–17 to 2020–21.

04

How does fire shape the forests?

Fire is natural in many forests, and some fires are
lit on purpose to reduce bushfire risk.

Bushfires swing sharply; planned burns stay steady.

This chart compares two types of fire. Unplanned fires, or bushfires, change a lot from year to year. They burnt 21 million hectares in 2012–13, but only 9 million the year after.

Planned burns are fires lit on purpose, usually in milder weather, to burn away dry leaves and fallen branches that could fuel a bushfire. Because they are controlled, they stay much steadier, covering 4–8 million hectares each year.

Click a legend entry to highlight one fire type.

The north burns every year. 2019–20 hit the south-east.

Each square shows how much of a state's forest burnt in a given year. Because it uses a percentage, states of different sizes can be compared fairly.

In the tropical north, fire happens every year. The Northern Territory burns between a quarter and a third of its forest annually.

The south-east burns less often, but its fires can be far worse. During the 2019–20 Black Summer, the Australian Capital Territory lost 63% of its forest, and New South Wales lost 25%, about 4.9 million hectares.

Shares use each state's 2023 forest area, so they are approximate.

The Black Summer fires spread down the south-east coast.

To show how the Black Summer spread, this map uses fire data from NASA satellites. Each time a satellite passes overhead and picks up the heat of a fire, it records a detection. This means one large fire can be counted many times.

There were more than 205,000 detections over the season, and 39% of them were in New South Wales. December 2019 was the worst month, with 37% of all detections.

Choose “Month by month” and press play to watch the fires move south. Detections include fires in grassland as well as forest. Each hexagon covers about 6,300 km², so counts can be compared as densities.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 3.1b (2024). Financial years run July to June. NASA FIRMS MODIS Collection 6.1 fire detections (MCD14ML), vegetation fires with confidence of 30 or more.

05

Who is paid to bring forests back?

A carbon credit scheme pays landholders to regrow or keep
forest. Each credit is one tonne of carbon dioxide.

Forest carbon credits come from two states, not the most forested ones.

In this map, each state is resized based on how many carbon credits its forest projects have earned. New South Wales and Queensland appear the largest, earning 87% of all forest credits between them.

Victoria and Tasmania, two of the most heavily forested states, almost disappear. Neither has any native forest regeneration projects.

In New South Wales, most credits come from avoided clearing, which pays landholders not to clear regrowth on their land.

Credits measure carbon, not forest area. Drag the slider to watch credits build up since 2013–14.

Paying for regrowth has taken over.

Avoided clearing pays landholders to keep forest they were allowed to clear. In 2014–15, this type of project earned most of the forest credits.

By 2025–26, the lines had crossed. Native forest regeneration, which lets cleared land grow back into forest, earned 9.5 million credits, or 77% of the total. Avoided clearing dropped to 1.0 million. This shows the scheme has shifted from keeping existing forest to growing forest back.

Compares the first year all three kinds earned credits with the latest full year. Plantings combines native tree plantings and commercial plantations. Click a line to highlight it.

Source: Clean Energy Regulator ACCU Scheme project register (as at 30 September 2026), active forest projects, credits to June 2026.

06

Who looks after the forests?

Beyond payments, some forest is set aside in reserves. How
much is protected, and who holds the rest?

Most forest sits outside the reserve system.

This diagram follows Australia's forest from who holds the land, on the left, to whether it is part of the National Reserve System, on the right. The National Reserve System is Australia's network of national parks and other protected areas.

Most forest, 68%, is on leasehold or private land. Leasehold land is public land that is rented out, often for grazing. Only 26% of forest is in the reserve system, which includes almost all conservation reserves and some private land.

Counting every measure, 37% is protected.

Reserves are not the only way forest is protected. All protection measures also include covenants, where landholders legally agree to protect the bush on their property, and protected zones inside public forests.

When all of these are counted, 37% of native forest is protected. The other 63% falls outside these measures, although other laws, such as limits on clearing, may still apply.

Protection varies widely between states.

Each line connects the share of a state's forest in reserves to its share under all protection measures.

The Australian Capital Territory protects 95% of its forest. Queensland, which has the most forest, protects only 24%.

Victoria has the largest gap, with 43% in reserves but 86% under all measures. This means half of its protected forest sits outside the reserve system.

The measures overlap, so do not add them. Click a legend entry to highlight one measure.

Source: ABARES Indicator 1.1c (2024), tables for 2020–21.

Conclusion

Cleared, burnt and paid to regrow.

Australia's forests are mostly eucalypt, and the largest share, 39%, grows in Queensland.

Clearing of previously uncleared forest has fallen 96%. Yet 155,000 hectares of regrowth were still re-cleared in 2020–21, even as credits for regrowing forest rose to 9.5 million a year.

Reserves guard against clearing, not fire. The Australian Capital Territory protects 95% of its forest, yet the Black Summer burnt 63% of it.

With 68% of native forest on leasehold and private land, its future depends less on reserves than on the people who own the land.
