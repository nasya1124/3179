01

Where do Australia's forests grow?

Tasmania is the most densely forested state.

Native forest covers half of Tasmania. The small Australian Capital Territory is higher still, at 55%.

The dry interior has little forest. It covers under 10% of South Australia and Western Australia.

Shading divides forest area by land area, so states of different sizes compare fairly.

But Queensland holds the most forest.

Queensland has 52 million hectares, or 39% of all native forest.

Tasmania falls to second-last. A small state can be densely forested yet hold little forest overall.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 1.1a.i (2023); ABS ASGS Edition 4 boundaries (2026).

02

What are the forests made of?

Three in four hectares are eucalypt.

Eucalypts cover 77% of native forest: 101 million hectares.

Next come acacia, known locally as wattle, at 8%, and melaleuca, or paperbark, at 5%. Rainforest is under 3%.

Five smaller types are grouped as “Other types”. Hover that slice to see them.

Some trees live in just one corner.

Mountain ash, the world's tallest flowering plant, is recorded almost only in Victoria and Tasmania.

Bunya pine clusters in south-east Queensland. Boab grows in the Kimberley, in the far north-west.

Circles count herbarium specimens per 1° grid cell, not living trees. Click a species in the legend to show only its records.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 1.1a.i (2023); Atlas of Living Australia herbarium records.

03

How much forest is lost and regained?

Uncleared forest is rarely cleared now. Regrowth is cleared instead.

Clearing of previously uncleared forest fell by 96%, from 605,000 hectares in 1989–90 to 22,000 in 2020–21.

But regrowth is often cleared again: re-clearing still removed 155,000 hectares in 2020–21.

Overall, forest area grew in only seven straight years, from 2008–09 to 2014–15.

Regrowth is forest measured growing back on cleared land. Click a legend entry to highlight one type.

A hardwood boom, then a decline.

Hardwood plantations grew tenfold in the 1990s and 2000s, peaking near 1 million hectares in 2008–09.

Since then they have shrunk by 31%. Softwood, mostly pine, has held steady at about 1 million hectares.

Drag across the lower strip to zoom. Click a legend entry or a band to highlight that type in both charts. Starts in 1973–74, when hardwood reporting became consistent.

Most states plant pine; Tasmania and the west plant gums.

About 55,000 hectares are planted or replanted each year. In Queensland and New South Wales, almost all of it is softwood.

Tasmania and the west plant about three-quarters hardwood, mostly eucalypts.

Totals at the right keep each state's size visible. Hover a bar for exact shares.

04

How does fire shape the forests?

Bushfires swing sharply; planned burns stay steady.

Unplanned fires burnt 21 million hectares in 2012–13, then 9 million the next year.

Planned burns reduce fuel. They cover 4–8 million hectares each year.

Click a legend entry to highlight one fire type.

The north burns every year. 2019–20 hit the south-east.

The Northern Territory burns a quarter to a third of its forest every year.

In the 2019–20 Black Summer, the Australian Capital Territory lost 63% of its forest. New South Wales lost 25%, about 4.9 million hectares.

Shares use each state's 2023 forest area, so they are approximate.

The Black Summer fires spread down the south-east coast.

Satellites detected fires more than 205,000 times over the season. New South Wales alone had 39% of them.

December 2019 was the worst month, with 37% of the season's detections.

Step through the months to watch the fires move south. Detections count fires in all vegetation, including grassland. Each hexagon covers about 6,300 km², so a count is also a density.

Sources: ABARES Indicator 3.1b (2024). Financial years run July to June. NASA FIRMS MODIS Collection 6.1 fire detections (MCD14ML), vegetation fires with confidence of 30 or more.

05

Who is paid to bring forests back?

Forest carbon credits come from two states, not the most forested ones.

Landholders earn carbon credits for regrowing or keeping forest. New South Wales and Queensland have earned 87% of them.

Victoria and Tasmania, among the most densely forested states, have no native regeneration projects at all.

In New South Wales, most credits come from avoided clearing: paying landholders not to clear regrowth.

Credits are tonnes of carbon dioxide the register reports as stored or avoided, not hectares of forest. Drag the slider to watch credits build up since 2013–14.

Paying for regrowth has taken over.

Credits issued each year grew from 1.4 million in 2013–14 to 12.5 million in 2025–26.

Avoided clearing led for the first three years. Native forest regeneration overtook it in 2016–17 and earned 77% of credits in 2025–26.

Credits for native tree plantings and commercial plantations are combined, as they are small. Click a legend entry to highlight one kind.

Source: Clean Energy Regulator ACCU Scheme project register (as at 30 September 2026), active forest projects, credits to June 2026.

06

Who looks after the forests?

Most forest sits outside the reserve system.

68% of native forest is on leasehold or private land. The National Reserve System, Australia's protected areas, holds only 26%.

Nearly all conservation reserves are inside it. Some private forest is included too.

Counting every measure, 37% is protected.

All protection measures include reserves, private covenants and protected zones in public forests.

The other 63% is not in that total. That does not mean it is unprotected.

Protection varies widely between states.

The Australian Capital Territory protects 95% of its forest; Queensland only 24%.

Victoria has the biggest gap: 43% in reserves, 86% with all measures.

The measures overlap, so do not add them. Click a legend entry to highlight one measure.

Source: ABARES Indicator 1.1c (2024), tables for 2020–21.

Conclusion

Vast, uneven and still changing.

Australia's forests are concentrated in the north and east, and eucalypts cover most of them.

Uncleared forest is now rarely cleared, but regrowth still is. Paid regrowth is growing fast, mostly in New South Wales and Queensland.

Fire reshapes them every year, and big seasons like 2019–20 reach far beyond the tropics.

Only 26% sits in formal reserves. Much of their future depends on private and leasehold land.
